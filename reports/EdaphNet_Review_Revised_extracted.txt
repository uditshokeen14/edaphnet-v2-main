[9] Heading 2 :: Abstract—Analytical laboratories support decision-making in agriculture, environmental monitoring, and clinical test-ing. Their reports are nevertheless vulnerable to sample swaps and numerical reporting errors. A sample swap as-signs a chemically valid result to the wrong sample identifier, whereas a numerical error alters a value during manual en-try or automated transfer. This paper presents EdaphNet, a batch-based quality assurance framework that combines an independent optical observation of each sample, acquired before chemical analysis, with probabilistic inference over sample permutations and typed numerical corruptions. The framework estimates likely errors, assigns suspicion scores, and proposes corrections. Evaluation uses 23,219 soil sam-ples from the Kellogg Soil Survey Laboratory (KSSL) and ICRAF–ISRIC repositories across four witness levels. On the KSSL benchmark, smartphone colour improves sample-swap detection by 10.3 percentage points over a supervised baseline. Laboratory Vis-NIR spectroscopy improves recall across all error types by 9.2 percentage points and achieves reported fault-attribution accuracy of 97–100%. The cali-bration experiments report that reusing calibration samples can undermine conformal false discovery rate control; using unique calibration scores restores empirical control in the reported setting. These findings indicate the potential of optical witnesses while defining important limits related to sensor quality, error composition, and calibration design.

The witness supplies evidence about the physical sample against which its report can be checked. It is a verification input rather than a replacement for laboratory chemistry.

It returns suspicion scores, likely fault types, and candidate corrected values. The calibration analysis also identifies repeated calibration observations as a source of failure in the reported experimental design.

[18] List Paragraph :: Introduction
[19] Body Text :: Analytical chemistry laboratories provide measurements for agriculture, environmental compliance, food safety, and health-care. In soil testing, pH, organic carbon, total nitrogen, cation exchange capacity (CEC), and nutrient concentrations inform fertiliser application, soil amendments, and carbon accounting. Errors may occur despite correctly calibrated instruments be-cause of sample labelling, rack movement, or data entry. The cited proficiency study reports that 28% of soil organic carbon observations from eight commercial agricultural laboratories fell outside the stated proficiency criteria [1].
[20] Body Text :: Two error mechanisms require separate treatment. Sample swaps assign one sample’s results to another sample’s identifier: the chemistry remains plausible, but the identity is incorrect. Numerical errors alter reported values through decimal shifts,

[25] List Paragraph :: Related Work
[26] Normal :: A. Optical Soil Sensing
[27] Body Text :: Vis-NIR diffuse reflectance spectroscopy, spanning 350–2500 nm, is widely used for non-destructive soil characterisa-tion [3, 4]. Mobile tools such as LandPKS combine smartphone imaging with colour references to estimate soil properties [5]. These tools primarily predict properties. EdaphNet instead uses optical observations as independent evidence for checking laboratory reports.
[28] Body Text :: 
[29] Normal :: B. Autoverification Methods
[30] Body Text :: Range-based autoverification is used in industrial and clinical environments [6, 7]. Multi-analyte checks can identify wrong-blood-in-tube events by comparing current measurements with previous patient results [2]. Such checks are less suitable for cross-sectional environmental batches, which may lack histori-cal measurements and contain swapped samples that still satisfy plausible property ranges.
[31] Normal :: C. Permutation Matching and Conformal Testing
[32] Body Text :: Genomic studies identify sample mix-ups by matching informa-tive genotype signatures [8]. Soil optical witnesses are noisier, and their predictive value varies by property. EdaphNet com-bines this evidence with a probabilistic model of laboratory results rather than treating it as an exact identifier. Conformal p-values, together with a suitable multiple-testing procedure, provide a framework for false discovery rate (FDR) control under the required assumptions [9, 10, 12]. Confident-learning approaches address label errors [11]. Here, the reported experi-ments highlight the need to examine how repeated calibration observations affect those assumptions.

[129] Body Text :: Initial experiments reported realised FDR between 57% and 92%. Diagnostic analysis attributed this failure to pseudo-replication: approximately 511 calibration soils were repeatedly sampled across 400 evaluation batches, producing repeated scores and undermining the intended calibration design. After restricting calibration to one score per unique sample, with ap-proximately 2,800 KSSL samples, the reported empirical FDR fell below 0.10. These observations emphasise that calibra-tion validity depends on sampling design and the relationship between calibration and evaluation data.

[209] List Paragraph :: Throughput
[210] Body Text :: Joint inference for a 24-well tray is reported to take 184 ms on an Intel Core i7 processor. This supports evaluating the method for integration into laboratory workflows in which witnesses are captured before destructive digestion and verification runs once reports become available. Deployment still requires validation on the target laboratory’s hardware, handling procedures, and calibration data.
[211] List Paragraph :: Conclusion
[212] Body Text :: EdaphNet combines optical measurements of physical samples with joint inference over sample permutations and numerical corruption. Evaluation on 23,219 samples shows that smart-phone colour can aid sample-swap detection. On the reported KSSL benchmark, laboratory Vis-NIR spectroscopy improves recall by 9.2 percentage points over the best baseline across error types and achieves fault-attribution accuracy of 97–100% in the spectrometer setting.
[213] Body Text :: The calibration experiments report failure when calibration observations are repeated and restoration of empirical control when unique scores are used. These findings emphasise careful calibration design rather than an unconditional FDR guarantee. Results remain specific to the datasets and evaluation condi-tions: RGB performance varies by property and dataset, while numerical corruption remains a relative weakness because hard scientific constraints are not explicitly encoded. Future work includes field validation using Indian agricultural soil trays and integration with real-time robotic handling systems.
