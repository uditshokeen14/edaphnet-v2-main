EdaphNet v2: Complete Project Summary

Checking soil laboratory reports with a cheap independent observation of the sample · status on 3 October 2026 · paper draft v0.2 · repository github.com/Sudeep-Sharma1/edaphnet-v2 (private)
In one paragraph. Soil test reports are sometimes wrong: a report can belong to another sample (a swap), or one number can be wrong (a value error, such as a ×10 slip). EdaphNet asks whether a cheap, independent look at the same soil, mainly its colour as a phone could record it, can tell a laboratory which reports to re-test and what went wrong. We built a model that checks a whole lab batch at once and tested it, with rules fixed in advance, on 23,219 real soils with planted errors. Result: colour helps find swaps (+0.10) but not wrong values (−0.08), so overall it did not beat the best existing method (the main pre-registered claim failed); a lab spectrometer did beat it (+0.09); and the model names the right error type 97–100% of the time. The work is publishable as an honest study of when a cheap witness is enough.

1. Problem and motivation

Soil reports drive fertiliser, liming and carbon decisions. Errors happen in two ways:

  • Swaps: samples are mislabelled or placed in the wrong tray position, so a farmer receives another soil's results. A swapped report looks perfectly normal, because it is a real report of a different soil, so lab-only checks cannot see it.
  • Value errors: decimal slips (×10, ÷10), unit mix-ups, swapped digits (0.16 → 0.61), swapped columns, or analytical failures.

Measurement disagreement is not rare: in one comparison of eight service labs, 28% of soil organic carbon results fell outside the proficiency-testing acceptable range (cited in the draft; to be verified). Labs control quality with reference samples and rules, but an individual farmer has no independent way to check their report. Re-testing everything is too expensive, so labs need to know which few reports to re-test.

2. Research objectives and questions

  1. Detection: rank reports so that re-testing the top 5% catches as many errors as possible.
  2. Attribution: say what went wrong (which sample it was swapped with, or which value and which kind of slip).
  3. Correction: propose the corrected value or the correct sample.
  4. Detectability: map which errors each witness strength can reveal (none → colour → spectrometer).
  5. Error control (secondary): flag reports with a guaranteed false-alarm rate (FDR 10%).

4. Research gaps

  • No method found that uses an independent observation of the same soil sample to jointly detect, explain and correct swaps and value errors across a lab batch.
  • No detectability map: which report errors a witness of a given strength can reveal.
  • No public benchmark of soil lab-report errors (LimeSoDa and REIN are the nearest; neither has lab errors).
  • No study of whether conformal false-alarm control stays valid with structured lab errors and realistic calibration data.

The "no prior work" statements still need the formal Scopus / Web of Science search (planned; HANDOVER.md step 3).

5. Proposed methodology

  1. Witness model. Each property is put on a standard scale (log or logit where needed). From the witness (colour, 10 visible bands, or the full spectrum) a gradient-boosted model predicts each property; the leftover errors give a full covariance, so the model knows how carbon, nitrogen and total carbon move together. Result: for each sample, a predicted distribution of its properties.
  2. Value-error library. ×10, ÷10, unit confusions, swapped adjacent digits of the printed number, swapped columns, and a gross multiplicative error over a grid. At most one corrupted value per report.
  3. Swap hypotheses. No swap; neighbours swapped; whole tray shifted by one or two places (rotations); any two samples swapped.
  4. Joint batch inference. For every report and every candidate sample, the model scores how well the printed report fits that sample's witness under each possible error. All swap hypotheses are scored together over the batch, exactly over the enumerated set.
  5. Output per report. Suspicion score = 1 − probability the report is clean; the most likely explanation (swap partner or error type); a proposed correction.
  6. Lab decision. Re-test the top 5% (primary). For error control: conformal p-values against clean calibration reports, then Benjamini–Hochberg at q = 0.10, using one score per distinct calibration sample.

What is real and what is simulated. The soils, lab values and spectra are real. The errors were planted by our code (a swap event in 20% of batches; value errors in 2.5% of other reports; about 5% erroneous reports in total), because only then do we know where the errors are. The "phone" colour was calculated from each lab spectrum, plus camera-like noise (ΔE 0, 2, 4, 8): a best case, since lab soil is dried and ground. Splits are by profile or location (train 50%, calibration 15%, test 35%), so test soils come from places never seen in training. Rebuilding the KSSL dataset from the downloaded files gives an identical result.

10. Results

10.1 What colour knows (R² on unseen locations)

KSSL: organic carbon 0.72 (0.70 with phone-like noise), nitrogen 0.55, total carbon 0.69. ICRAF: organic carbon 0.37, pH 0.27, Ca 0.23, clay 0.16, CEC 0.11, K 0.07, sand 0.02. A spectrometer explains 0.39–0.90 of all properties. Colour mainly knows carbon.

10.2 Simulation

The method beat all baselines only with a moderately informative witness (e.g. 0.65 vs 0.42 of errors caught at FDR 0.10 when colour explains ~66% of carbon); with a weak witness the gain was ≈ 0. The gain came almost entirely from swaps.

10.4 Why colour did not win: swaps vs wrong values (exploratory)

With a good-phone colour witness, EdaphNet found +0.103 [0.064, 0.139] more swaps and −0.082 [−0.116, −0.050] fewer wrong values than the supervised baseline; at an equal mix the two cancel. Wrong values were lost mainly on gross errors (0.03 vs 0.26) and ×10 slips (0.68 vs 0.80). The swap hypotheses and the witness create the swap advantage (+0.39, +0.40); the error library trades swap sensitivity (−0.07) for slightly better value detection (+0.02); the structured swap prior had no effect.

10.5 Who wins depends on the error mix (exploratory)

Re-ranking the stored scores under other mixes: with colour, EdaphNet beats the best baseline once 60–70% of errors are swaps (e.g. +0.066 [0.037, 0.095] at 90%) and loses when few are (−0.054 at 10%); with a spectrometer it wins from 30% swaps. It beats the simple swap matcher at every mix. A lab that knows its own error mix can read off which checker to use.

10.6 Other findings

  • Explaining errors (clearest advantage): correct error type in 97–100% of flagged value errors (KSSL, spectrometer; pre-registered A3), vs 24–29% for rule-based repair; 89–98% on ICRAF. With colour, too few value errors were flagged to evaluate.
  • Unknown error types (5 seeds): same pattern: swaps +0.103 [0.069, 0.137], wrong values −0.057 [−0.078, −0.036], overall +0.027 [0.009, 0.047] (colour, random batches).
  • Gross errors: a real limitation, not a bug. EdaphNet ranks them fairly high but explains them as digit slips, and it does not use the rule "organic carbon ≤ total carbon" that the baselines exploit.
  • False-alarm control: fails silently when calibration samples are reused (realised FDR 0.57 → 0.00 after de-duplication). Its power depends on calibration size relative to how many reports are tested at once: Benjamini–Hochberg over m reports can reject only when at least m/(q(N+1)) reports reach the smallest possible p-value (≈ 5 for a pool of 1,200, fewer than 1 for one batch). Testing one batch at a time restored power to 0.19–0.25 at FDR ≈ 0.10 (exploratory).
  • Detectability: swaps go from ~3% found without a witness to 40–55% with colour and up to 87% with a spectrometer; digit transpositions and moderate gross errors stay hard for every method.

15. Limitations

  1. Errors were planted, not observed; the real mix of swaps and wrong values is unknown.
  2. Colour was calculated from lab spectra of dried soil, a best case for a phone photo.
  3. KSSL properties are all carbon-related, which favours colour.
  4. B5 was trained on the true planted error mix, an advantage it would not have in practice.
  5. Several results are exploratory (not pre-registered) and need confirmation on new data.
  6. No Indian soils yet.
