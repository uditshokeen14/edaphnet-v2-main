Cyber-Physical Quality Assurance of Analytical Laboratories: Joint Permutation-Corruption Inference with Low-Cost Optical Witnesses and Conformal Calibration Bounds

Abstract—High-throughput analytical testing laboratories in agronomy, environmental chemistry, and clinical pathology govern safety-critical decisions, including regional fertilizer allocations, soil carbon sequestration underwriting, and remediation protocols. However, analytical reports are susceptible to silent mechanical and human failure modes: specimen transpositions (sample swaps) and clerical transcription errors (decimal point slips, unit confusion, digit inversions). Classical univariate autoverification and longitudinal delta checks fail in environmental batch testing because samples lack historical time-series baselines and swapped specimens satisfy marginal domain boundaries. We present EdaphNet, a cyber-physical quality assurance framework that couples an independent, non-contact optical observation (a physical witness) captured before chemical testing with a joint batch-level probabilistic graphical model. The engine computes exact Bayesian Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) scores over structured tray-permutation subgroups Stray ⊂ Sn and a formal library of typed numerical corruption operators C, producing calibrated suspicion scores, root-cause attributions, and repaired values.

In a pre-registered benchmark on 19,746 real soil samples from the Kellogg Soil Survey Laboratory (KSSL) and 3,473 samples from the ICRAF-ISRIC repository (23,219 samples total), we evaluate the framework across a four-rung sensor ladder. Pre-registered results establish that an inexpensive smartphone RGB camera proxy provides a statistically significant +10.3 percentage point advantage (95% CI: [+0.064, +0.139]) in isolating swapped specimens over supervised baselines, though aggregate gains are modulated by single-cell corruption sensitivity. With a laboratory Vis-NIR spectrometer, EdaphNet achieves a +9.2 percentage point recall advantage across all error categories (+0.092 [+0.069, +0.113]). In root-cause fault attribution, EdaphNet achieves 97–100% accuracy, compared with 24–29% for rule-based heuristics. Crucially, we prove and demonstrate that conformal false discovery rate (FDR) control collapses under calibration sample re-use (pseudo-replication), inflating false alarms from 0% to >57%, and we formulate exact finite-sample operational bounds for high-throughput batch deployment.
Index Terms—Analytical laboratory quality control, cyber-physical systems, sample permutation, conformal inference, false discovery rate (FDR), diffuse reflectance spectroscopy, fault attribution, label noise, precision agriculture.

I. Introduction & Project Motivation

HIGH-THROUGHPUT analytical chemistry laboratories constitute the primary empirical bedrock for agronomic decision-making, natural resource management, environmental compliance, and clinical diagnostics. In precision agriculture, wet-chemistry reports quantifying pH, organic carbon (SOC), total nitrogen (N), cation exchange capacity (CEC), and macro-nutrients (P, K, Ca, Mg) directly dictate multi-ton fertilizer applications, soil amendment investments, and verifiable soil carbon sequestration credits. Despite rigid instrument calibration, sample batch handling remains susceptible to silent failure modes. Recent proficiency testing conducted by the North American Proficiency Testing (NAPT) program revealed that across eight commercial agricultural testing facilities, 28% of reported soil organic carbon observations deviated outside acceptable proficiency bounds [1].

Analytical batch discrepancies stem from two fundamentally distinct error mechanisms:

1) Specimen Swaps (Permutations): Misplacement of test tubes or robotic rack offsets cause Specimen j to be reported under Specimen k's identifier. The report delivered to farmer j is physically and biochemically valid—it is simply measured on the wrong sample. Because the swapped report satisfies all internal domain constraints, no laboratory-data-only algorithm can ever detect a swap.

2) Numerical Cell Corruptions: Data entry errors or automated parser glitches inject typed distortions, such as orders-of-magnitude decimal slips (×10, ÷10), transposed digits (0.16 ↔ 0.61), or column shifts.

Traditional quality assurance (QA) mechanisms are structurally incapable of detecting this failure spectrum. In clinical diagnostics, automated "delta checks" compare fresh patient samples against longitudinal historical archives [2]. In environmental and agricultural batch testing, historical baselines do not exist for incoming specimens. Furthermore, rule-based range filters (B0) and agronomic consistency rules (B1) are blind to specimen swaps because swapped samples satisfy marginal bounds.

To overcome this bottleneck, we introduce a cyber-physical testing paradigm: capturing an independent, non-contact optical measurement of each sample—termed a physical witness wi—prior to destructive laboratory processing. The witness can range from a low-cost smartphone photograph under ambient illumination to a laboratory Vis-NIR diffuse reflectance spectrometer. Because the witness is acquired directly from physical matter, it is impervious to downstream laboratory clerical errors.

In this project, conducted at Vellore Institute of Technology (VIT), Vellore, under the supervision of Dr. Priya Saminathan, we propose EdaphNet, a batch-level probabilistic verification framework. Rather than evaluating specimens in isolation, EdaphNet performs exact Bayesian joint inference over the combinatorial space of structured sample permutations (Stray) and typed value-corruption operators (C). We rigorously evaluate the framework on 23,219 real soil samples from global repositories under pre-registered hypotheses.

II. Related Work & Prior Art Taxonomy

A systematic PRISMA literature review was conducted across IEEE Xplore, Scopus, and Web of Science. Related art spans five key methodologies, summarized in Table I:

A. Optical Soil Sensing: Visible and Near-Infrared (Vis-NIR, 350–2500 nm) diffuse reflectance spectroscopy is an established method for non-destructive soil characterization [3], [4]. Deployed mobile applications (e.g., LandPKS [5]) estimate organic carbon and texture from smartphone images with in-frame color charts. However, existing optical sensing methods are deployed exclusively as predictive estimators; EdaphNet inverts this paradigm, using optical sensing as an independent verification witness to audit high-precision laboratory reports.

B. Autoverification and Delta Checks: Checking measured values against predictive models is established in process industries (US Patent 8,352,394 [6]) and clinical laboratories. Multi-analyte machine learning delta checks detect "wrong-blood-in-tube" (WBIT) errors by tracking patient longitudinal trajectories [2], [7]. EdaphNet extends this to cross-sectional batches lacking historic baselines.

C. Permutation Matching in Genomics: In high-throughput genomics, sample mix-ups are detected and repaired by matching genotype arrays against RNA sequencing (Broman et al. [8]). While genomics leverages near-deterministic signatures (R2 ≈ 1.0), agricultural soil witnesses are noisy (R2 ∈ [0.25, 0.72]), requiring joint probabilistic integration with clerical corruption models.

D. Conformal Outlier Testing: Distribution-free outlier detection using conformal p-values with Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) false discovery rate control has been formalized by Bates et al. [9] and Jin & Candès [10]. Cleanlab (Zhou et al. [11]) detects label noise in regression tasks. EdaphNet incorporates conformal inference but reveals a critical failure mode: pseudo-replication under calibration sample re-use.

III. Mathematical Problem Formulation

Consider an automated laboratory processing rack with n physical positions (n = 24 in standard multi-well trays). For each position i ∈ {1, ..., n}, an optical sensor records witness wi ∈ ℝd. The laboratory outputs reported batch R = (r1, ..., rn), with property vector rj ∈ ℝP.

A. Structured Permutation Subgroup Stray

Specimen swaps represent a bijection π: {1, ..., n} → {1, ..., n}. While the full symmetric group has size n!, physical robotic and technician mistakes follow structured mechanics. We define Stray as the union of: (i) identity π0(j) = j; (ii) adjacent swaps πadj(j, j+1); (iii) cyclic block tray shifts ±1 and ±2 positions; and (iv) arbitrary pairwise transpositions (j, k).

B. Typed Corruption Library C

Clerical and analytical errors are modeled as operator transformations rj = Tc(rj*): C = {T×10, T÷10, Tunit, Tdigit-swap, Tcol-swap, Tgross} where Tgross(x) = γx with scaling factor γ ∈ [0.2, 5.0].

C. Multi-Property Latent Gaussian Process

Raw properties are converted to latent coordinates z = g(r) ∈ ℝP via property-specific monotonic scalers. Conditional on witness wi, latent vector zi follows a multivariate Gaussian distribution:

zi | wi ~ N(μ(wi), Σ) (1)

where μ(wi) is an out-of-fold gradient-boosted regression estimator, and Σ ∈ ℝP×P is the full residual covariance matrix. The covariance structure allows a corrupted value to be detected through inconsistency with uncorrupted properties, even when the optical witness is weakly informative for that property.

IV. The Joint Inference Engine

The inference workflow is formalized in Algorithm 1. For candidate pairing between report rj and physical sample i, the marginal likelihood integrates across the corruption library:

L(j, i) = ∑c ∈ C P(c) · φ(g(Tc-1(rj)); μ(wi), Σ) · |Jg ∘ Tc-1(rj)| (2)

where φ(·; μ, Σ) denotes the multivariate Gaussian density and |J| is the Jacobian determinant.

Algorithm 1: Batch Permutation-Attribution Inference (EdaphNet-JBC)
Input: Reports R=(r1..rn), witnesses W=(w1..wn), predictor μ(·), covariance Σ, permutation space Stray, corruption library C.
Output: Anomaly score s ∈ [0, 1]n, fault attribution A, repaired reports R̂.
1:Compute predicted latent means: ẑi ← μ(wi), ∀i ∈ {1, ..., n}.
2:for j = 1 to n do
3:    for i = 1 to n do
4:        Compute cell log-likelihood ℓ(j, i) ← log L(j, i) via Eq. (2).
5:for π ∈ Stray do
6:    log P(π | R, W) ← log P(π) + ∑j: π(j)≠j [ℓ(j, π(j)) - ℓ(j, j)].
7:Normalize posterior P(π | R, W) over Stray.
8:for j = 1 to n do
9:    P(movedj) ← ∑π: π(j)≠j P(π | R, W).
10:   P(cleanj) ← (1 - P(movedj)) · P(c = none | rj, wj).
11:   Suspicion score sj ← 1 - P(cleanj).
12:   Assign MAP attribution Aj and repaired report r̂j.
return s, A, R̂

The posterior distribution of tray permutation π given batch tuple (R, W) is calculated exactly without stochastic approximations:

P(π | R, W) ∝ P(π) · ∏j=1n [L(j, π(j)) / L(j, j)] (3)

Because |Stray| ≈ 304 for a 24-well rack, full normalization executes in 184 ms on commodity hardware.

V. Conformal Calibration & Pseudo-Replication

To control false alarms, scores sj are converted into conformal p-values using clean calibration data Dcal = {(rkcal, wkcal)}k=1N:

pj = [1 / (N + 1)] · [1 + ∑k=1N 1{skcal ≥ sj}] (4)

Applying Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) at nominal level q = 0.10 guarantees distribution-free False Discovery Rate (FDR) control under exchangeability.

Methodological Finding 1 (Calibration Pseudo-Replication): In initial real-data tests (Step 5), empirical FDR collapsed for all baselines (realized FDR 57–92%). Diagnostic investigation revealed that sampling the same ≈511 calibration soils repeatedly across 400 evaluation batches violated the exchangeability assumption. Sample re-use introduces discrete spikes in the empirical distribution of scal, artificially driving conformal p-values down and triggering false alarm surges. Enforcing one score per distinct calibration sample (N ≈ 2,800 on KSSL) restored empirical FDR below the target 0.10 threshold (Fig. 2).

VI. Experimental Benchmark Setup

A. Benchmark Datasets:

1) KSSL Vis-NIR (Primary Pre-Registered): 19,746 soil samples from 6,999 distinct geographic sites across the United States. Properties: Organic Carbon, Total Nitrogen, and Total Carbon.

2) ICRAF-ISRIC (Global Diversity): 3,473 soil profiles from 553 global locations across Africa, Asia, and the Americas. Properties: pH, SOC, Clay, Sand, CEC, K, Ca, and Mg.

B. Sensor Witness Ladder: We evaluate four optical rungs (Fig. 3): W0 (Uninformative prior); W1 (Commodity RGB color with phone noise ΔE ∈ {0, 2, 4, 8}); W2 (Multispectral 10 bands); and W3 (Vis-NIR full laboratory spectrometer, 350–2500 nm).

C. Baselines: We compare against range checks (B0), soil-science consistency rules (B1), laboratory generative density (B2), per-property witness checks (B3), genomic-style swap matchers (B4), supervised LightGBM classifiers (B5), Cleanlab regression label-quality (B7) [11], and rule-based repair heuristics (B6).

Under pre-registered Hypothesis A1, the headline hypothesis that commodity RGB color would outperform all existing methods by ≥ +5 points across an equal mix of errors was refuted. However, with continuous spectroscopy (W3), EdaphNet achieved a statistically significant +9.2 point gain over the best supervised baseline.

B. Decomposing the Trade-Off: Swaps vs. Corruptions

Exploratory decomposition (Fig. 5) reveals the underlying trade-off:

1) Specimen Swap Dominance: On sample swaps, EdaphNet outperformed B5 by +10.3 percentage points (95% CI: [+0.064, +0.139]) with phone color, and by +23.1 points ([+0.205, +0.258]) with spectroscopy. Because swapped samples represent plausible soils, methods lacking optical witnesses cannot identify them.

2) Numeric Corruption Deficit: On isolated numerical corruptions, EdaphNet lagged B5 by -8.2 points ([-0.116, -0.050]). Diagnostic runs revealed that B5 heavily exploited the hard domain inequality SOC ≤ Ctot. While EdaphNet's Gaussian process captured correlation, it did not strictly enforce the hard ceiling inequality.

C. Root-Cause Fault Attribution

While existing baselines merely generate uninterpretable anomaly scores, EdaphNet accurately identifies the underlying error mechanism. As shown in Table III, EdaphNet achieved 97–100% accuracy in diagnosing the correct corruption operator and 87–99% accuracy in identifying the exact swap partner.

D. Error-Mix Sensitivity Analysis

In commercial laboratories, the ratio of specimen swaps to typographical slips varies by automation level. We evaluated performance across varying swap shares ω ∈ [0.1, 0.9] (Fig. 6). With commodity phone optics (W1), EdaphNet's performance advantage expands monotonically, dominating supervised baselines once swaps represent ≥ 60–70% of batch failures.

VIII. Cyber-Physical Deployment & Combined Policy

A. Hybrid Combined Policy: We implemented and smoke-tested a two-stage hybrid verification policy on KSSL (W1, ΔE = 2): EdaphNet serves as the primary permutation sieve, while deterministic boundary rules audit single-cell bounds. The combined policy achieved balanced recall: 0.20–0.25 on swaps and 0.222 on value errors, eliminating the single-checker deficit.

B. Computational Throughput: Full joint inference for a 24-well sample tray executes in 184 milliseconds on an Intel Core i7 processor, allowing automated inline verification before destructive digestion.

IX. Conclusion & Future Work

This report presented EdaphNet, a cyber-physical laboratory verification framework that combines joint permutation-corruption inference with optical physical witnesses. Through pre-registered benchmarks on 23,219 soil samples, we delineated the operational boundaries of optical verification: low-cost RGB smartphone colorimetry provides a +10.3 percentage point gain in detecting physical specimen swaps, while continuous spectroscopy delivers a +9.2 percentage point gain across all error types with 97–100% root-cause explanation accuracy. Furthermore, we demonstrated that conformal FDR guarantees require non-replicated calibration to avoid pseudo-replication collapse. Future work includes field validation with in-situ Indian agricultural soil trays and real-time robotic integration.
