EdaphNet

Checking soil laboratory reports with an independent sample witness: what has been done, what was found, and what remains

Date: 2 October 2026 · Project folder: edaphnet-v2 · Repository: https://github.com/Sudeep-Sharma1/edaphnet-v2 Purpose of this report: a complete, plain-language account of what has been done, what was found (including what failed), and what remains. Every number below comes from a committed run record or document, named in brackets.

1. The project in one paragraph

Soil test reports are sometimes wrong: a sample is swapped with another one (the farmer gets someone else's results), or a single value is corrupted (a ×10 decimal slip, a unit mix-up, swapped digits or columns). Nobody checks this for the end user, and re-testing everything is expensive. EdaphNet asks whether a cheap, independent observation of the same soil sample (a witness, mainly its colour as a phone camera would see it) can tell a laboratory which reports to re-test and what went wrong. The core method is a joint model that checks a whole laboratory batch at once, considering possible swaps and a library of error types together.

6. Key results

6.1 Simulation (doc 13, synthetic)

The method beats all baselines only when the witness is moderately informative (e.g. +20 to +25 points in errors found at 10% false alarms when colour explains ~66% of organic carbon), not with a weak witness. Its gain comes almost entirely from finding swaps.

Verdict: A1 (main claim) failed; A1-c, A2 (false alarms ≤ 12%) and A3 (attribution) passed. Note on A2: with a colour witness the method flagged almost nothing (power ≈ 0), so A2 passed by staying silent at those settings; only the spectrometer setting combined low false alarms with real power.

6.4 Other findings

  • Error explanation: right error type 89–98% (ICRAF) and 97–100% (KSSL, spectrometer), vs 24–51% for rule-based repair.
  • Which errors can be found (doc 19): with a colour witness, swaps go from ~3% to ~40–55% found; single wrong values are found better by simple checks; digit transpositions and moderate analytical errors stay hard for every method.
  • Calibration lesson (D-017): reliable false-alarm control needs thousands of distinct verified reports; reusing calibration samples silently breaks it.

9. Reproducibility

Every experiment writes a run record (configuration, code version, environment, results). Seeds are fixed and runs can be resumed with identical results. The pass/fail rules and the verdict script were committed before the data they judge. Data licences and attribution are in the README.

11. Honest bottom line

The method works well when the witness is strong (spectrometer): it finds more errors than existing methods and explains them correctly. A phone-grade colour photo, at least as approximated from laboratory spectra, is not enough to beat the best existing method on average. The publishable contribution is therefore the careful, pre-registered study of when a cheap witness is enough, together with the calibration lesson and the explanation accuracy, unless real phone photos (step 6) or the combined policy change that picture.
